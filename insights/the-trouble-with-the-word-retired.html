The Trouble With the Word “Retired”

“What do you do?” gets harder to answer after a career ends. The label you choose shapes how you spend your days — so try on a few.

For decades, when someone asked, “What do you do?” the answer was easy. You were a teacher, a surgeon, an engineer, a business owner. The job was a shorthand for who you were, and you didn’t have to think much about it. Identity in retirement starts with a small but surprisingly weighty question: what do you call yourself now?

Why “Retired” Doesn’t Always Feel Right

The word retired carries baggage. For some people, it sounds like a finish line. For others, it feels like a label that flattens decades of accomplishment into one word. Some retirees avoid the term entirely because it doesn’t capture what they’re actively doing with their days.

You hear all kinds of alternatives. Some people call themselves investors, since managing a portfolio is real work. Others describe themselves by what they’re now doing rather than what they used to do. The label matters less than the fact that they’ve chosen it for themselves.

Language Shapes Your Reality

The words we use about ourselves shape how we approach the day. Calling yourself retired can inadvertently suggest you’re done. Calling yourself a writer, even if you’ve only just started, suggests you’re in the middle of writing something. That distinction influences how you spend your time.

You’re not avoiding the fact that you’ve left a career, but you are choosing language that reflects what’s currently true about your life. Most retirees aren’t done. They’re just doing different things.

Permission to Try on a Few Labels

There’s no rule that says you have to pick one identity and stick with it. Some of the most contented retirees describe themselves differently depending on the room. At a dinner party, they’re a gardener or a painter. At a community meeting, they’re a volunteer. With grandchildren, they’re whatever the kids call them.

Trying on a few labels is also part of figuring out what genuinely interests you in this stage of life. You might call yourself a beginner cellist for six months, then a hiker for the next year. None of it has to be permanent.

The Power of Choosing for Yourself

Redefining yourself in retirement is an ongoing process. Pay attention to what you’re doing and give it language that fits. The retirees who seem most at ease with this stage of life tend to share one thing: they decide what to call themselves, rather than letting a single word from their old life follow them around.
